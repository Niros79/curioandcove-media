How to Start Journaling: 30 Prompts for Beginners

Published September 30, 2026 – 7-minute read

Journaling doesn't need a beautiful notebook, perfect handwriting or an hour of free time. It needs five minutes and something to write about. This guide gives you both.

Why journaling is worth a try

Writing things down slows your thoughts to the speed of your hand. Worries that feel huge in your head often look smaller and more manageable on paper. Many people also find that a regular journal helps them notice patterns: what drains them, what lifts them, and what they keep coming back to.

It's also private. There's no audience, no likes and no wrong answers, which is rare these days.

How to start (and actually keep going)

  • Pick any notebook. A cheap one is often better, because you won't worry about "ruining" it. A notes app works too.
  • Keep it short. Five minutes or half a page is enough. Stopping while you still want to write makes it easier to come back tomorrow.
  • Attach it to something you already do. With your morning coffee, or in bed before you turn off the light.
  • Don't edit. Spelling, grammar and neatness don't matter. Write the way you think.
  • Use a prompt when you're stuck. That's what the list below is for.
  • Miss a day? Just continue. A journal isn't a streak. It waits for you.

30 journal prompts for beginners

Getting to know yourself

  1. What does a genuinely good day look like for me, from morning to night?
  2. What am I curious about right now?
  3. When do I feel most like myself?
  4. What is something I believed five years ago that I don't believe anymore?
  5. Which three words would a close friend use to describe me? Do I agree?
  6. What do I need more of this month, and what do I need less of?

Gratitude and small joys

  1. Three small things that went right today.
  2. A person who made my life easier recently, and how.
  3. A smell, song or taste that instantly makes me happy.
  4. Something I own that I'm quietly grateful for.
  5. A moment from this week I'd like to remember in ten years.
  6. What is something my body did for me today?

The changing season

  1. What do I want to let go of as the year winds down?
  2. What does "cozy" mean to me, and how can I bring more of it into my evenings?
  3. What did this past summer teach me?
  4. Which habit would I like to carry into the new year?
  5. Describe the view from my window right now, in detail.
  6. What would make the next three months feel calm and full?

Goals and next steps

  1. If fear weren't a factor, what would I try this year?
  2. What is one small step I can take this week toward something I want?
  3. What is a skill I'd love to learn, and why?
  4. What does "enough" look like for me?
  5. Where am I spending time that doesn't match what I value?
  6. What would future me thank me for starting today?

Letting go and starting fresh

  1. What is weighing on me that I haven't said out loud?
  2. A mistake I made, and what I'd tell a friend who made the same one.
  3. What am I holding onto that no longer fits?
  4. Write a short letter to myself one year ago.
  5. What can I forgive myself for today?
  6. What am I looking forward to, even a little?

Make it a ritual

Some people like to pair journaling with a small ritual: a cup of tea, a candle, a few slow breaths before they begin. Others like to journal with the moon, setting intentions at the new moon and reflecting at the full moon. There's no right way. Try a few and keep what feels good.

Journaling can be a helpful tool for reflection, but it isn't a replacement for professional support. If you're going through a hard time, talking to a doctor or a counsellor is a good step.

A small ritual makes the habit stick: the same chair, the same notebook and a warm drink. Quick recap

  • Five minutes a day in any notebook is enough.
  • Attach it to a habit you already have and don't edit as you write.
  • Use a prompt when you're stuck, and don't worry about missed days.
